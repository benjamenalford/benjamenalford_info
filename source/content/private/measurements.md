## 🔧 Needed Measurements — How to Achieve Them

### HIGH PRIORITY — Directly affects bike setup

| # | Measurement | Why It Matters | How to Measure | Equipment Needed | Solo? | Typical Range |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Sit bone width** | Determines saddle width — the single most important saddle choice. Wrong width = numbness, pain, poor support | **Cardboard method:** Place a piece of corrugated cardboard on a hard flat surface (bench or firm chair). Sit on it in a slight forward lean (mimicking riding position). Stand up — you'll see two dark indentations where your sit bones pressed. Measure center-to-center between the two deepest points. Add ~2 cm for saddle width (saddle should be slightly wider than sit bones). | Corrugated cardboard, ruler/tape measure, hard flat surface | ✅ Yes | 100–150 mm (10–15 cm) |
| 2 | **Femur length** (greater trochanter to lateral knee joint) | Determines saddle fore/aft (setback). Longer femur = saddle further back. Two people with the same inseam can have very different femur lengths | **Method A (direct):** Stand with feet shoulder-width. Find the greater trochanter — the bony bump on the outside of your upper thigh at the hip joint (it's the top of your femur, roughly at the level of your hip crease). Find the lateral knee joint line — the gap on the outside of your knee, just below the kneecap. Measure straight-line distance between these two points. **Method B (seated):** Sit on a flat chair, back against wall, knee bent at 90°. Measure from the front edge of the seat (behind your knee) to the front of your kneecap = tibia length. Then femur ≈ inseam − tibia − foot height (~7–8 cm). | Tape measure, flat chair (for Method B) | ✅ Yes (Method B easier) | 38–48 cm |
| 3 | **Hamstring flexibility** (straight leg raise angle) | Quantifies your "very tense" hamstrings — directly affects saddle height and forward lean tolerance. Gives your fitter a hard number instead of a subjective description | **Supine straight leg raise:** Lie on your back on the floor, legs flat. Have someone raise one leg slowly with the knee kept straight (locked). Stop when you feel significant resistance / the opposite leg starts to lift or the knee begins to bend. Measure the angle between the raised leg and the floor using a protractor or phone app (clinometer). Test both legs. | Floor, helper, protractor or phone clinometer app | ❌ Helper recommended | <60° = very tight, 60–80° = moderate, 80–90° = good |
| 4 | **Ankle dorsiflexion range** (knee-to-wall test) | Quantifies your calf tightness — affects pedal stroke mechanics and cleat position. With tight calves, you may pedal toe-down, which reduces power and increases calf strain | **Knee-to-wall test:** Kneel facing a wall with one foot forward, foot flat on the floor. Keeping your heel firmly on the ground, slide your forward knee toward the wall. Measure the distance from your big toe to the wall at the point where your heel just starts to lift off the ground. Larger distance = better dorsiflexion. Test both sides. | Wall, tape measure or ruler | ✅ Yes | <10 cm = limited, 10–13 cm = moderate, >13 cm = good |
| 5 | **Hip flexor flexibility** (Thomas test) | Tight hip flexors limit how far you can fold forward over the bike — directly affects how low/aero you can go comfortably | **Modified Thomas test:** Lie on your back on the edge of a bed or table, with one leg hanging off the side. Pull the other knee firmly to your chest and hold it there. The hanging leg's angle from horizontal = your hip flexor flexibility. If the thigh rises above horizontal, your hip flexors are tight. Measure the angle of the hanging thigh from horizontal. Test both sides. | Bed or table edge, helper to measure angle, protractor or phone clinometer | ❌ Helper recommended | Thigh below horizontal = good, at horizontal = moderate, above horizontal = tight |

### MEDIUM PRIORITY — Refines the fit

| # | Measurement | Why It Matters | How to Measure | Equipment Needed | Solo? | Typical Range |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 6 | **Thoracic spine rotation** | Determines how well you can rotate your upper body — affects reach to handlebars and ability to look up the road while in a low position | **Seated rotation test:** Sit upright in a chair with arms crossed on chest, feet flat on floor. Without moving your hips, rotate your torso as far as possible to one side. Have someone measure the angle from your starting forward-facing position. Note how far around you can see. Test both directions. | Chair, helper, protractor or phone clinometer | ❌ Helper recommended | 30–45° per side = normal; <30° = limited |
| 7 | **Shoulder external rotation** | Affects how comfortably you can reach the hoods/drops without wrist strain | **External rotation test:** Stand with elbows at your sides, bent at 90°, forearms pointing forward. Keeping elbows pinned to your sides, rotate forearms outward as far as possible. Measure the angle from the starting position (forearms forward) to the maximum outward position. Test both sides. | Helper, protractor or phone clinometer | ❌ Helper recommended | 70–90° = normal; <70° = limited |
| 8 | **Q-angle** (hip-knee-ankle alignment) | Determines how much your knees track inward/outward — affects cleat shimming and pedal stance width | **Q-angle measurement:** Stand naturally with feet shoulder-width apart. Find your ASIS (anterior superior iliac spine — the bony points on the front of your hip bones). Measure the angle formed by: a line from ASIS to the center of your kneecap, to the center of your ankle bone (lateral malleolus). Use a goniometer or have someone take a photo from the front and measure the angle. | Goniometer or camera + protractor, helper | ❌ Helper recommended | 12–17° for men; >17° may need wider pedal stance |
| 9 | **Foot arch height** | Determines if you need custom footbeds/insoles for cycling shoes — high arches without support lead to hot spots and numbness | **Wet footprint test:** Wet the sole of your foot and stand normally on a paper bag or piece of cardboard. Step off and look at the footprint. **Low arch (flat):** nearly the entire foot is visible. **Normal arch:** the arch area shows about half the width. **High arch:** the arch area shows very little or is completely absent. Classify as low, normal, or high. | Paper bag or cardboard, water | ✅ Yes | Low / Normal / High |

### 🎯 Priority Recommendations for Your Profile

Given your specific build (short torso, long arms, stiff lumbar, tight hamstrings/calves), the **top 3 measurements that will most help a bike fitter** are:

1. **Sit bone width** (#1) — essential for saddle selection, easy to measure at home
2. **Hamstring flexibility — straight leg raise** (#3) — turns "very tense" into a number your fitter can work with
3. **Ankle dorsiflexion — knee-to-wall** (#4) — quantifies your calf tightness for cleat and saddle height decisions

These three take about **10 minutes total** and would give a fitter (or you, if doing DIY) significantly more to work with.
